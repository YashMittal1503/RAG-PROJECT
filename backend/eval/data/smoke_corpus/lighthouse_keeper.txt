The Lighthouse at Gull Point

I came to Gull Point in the autumn of 1931, carrying one suitcase and a letter of appointment from the Harbour Board. The lighthouse stood at the end of a narrow spit of rock, and the old keeper, Mr. Thaddeus Crane, met me at the foot of the stairs with a lantern in one hand and a pipe in the other.

He did not shake my hand. He looked at my boots, then at the sky, and said that a storm would come before Sunday. He was right, as I would learn he nearly always was.

For the first week I polished brass, trimmed wicks and wrote the weather into the log every four hours. Crane spoke very little. In the evenings he read from a battered copy of a seafaring almanac and sometimes muttered about his daughter, Marigold, who had gone to the city and stopped writing.

On the eighth day the supply boat arrived. The boatman, a cheerful man named Pellow, tossed a sack of flour onto the jetty and called up to me, "Morning, Elias! Has the old man worked you to the bone yet?" I told him that he had not, which was a lie we both enjoyed.

The storm came on Saturday night, a day earlier than Crane had promised. Waves broke over the lower gallery and the glass of the lantern room rattled in its frame. At two in the morning the clockwork that turned the great lens jammed. Crane and I took turns cranking it by hand until dawn, and neither of us said a word about being tired.

In the morning Crane handed me his own key to the lantern room. "You'll want this," he said. "Marigold had the other one, once." It was the closest thing to praise I ever heard from him.

When the Harbour Board inspector visited in November, he wrote in his report that the light at Gull Point had not failed once during the storm. Crane read the report twice, folded it into his almanac, and asked me to put the kettle on.
